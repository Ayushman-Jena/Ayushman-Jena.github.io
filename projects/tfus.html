Transcranial Focused Ultrasound Stimulation Device

Ayushman Jena To be added 5 min read Neurostimulation Device Sponsored by DRDO Life Sciences Research Board

Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) is an emerging technology that enables non-invasive delivery of acoustic energy to specific regions of the brain. Unlike conventional neuromodulation techniques that rely on electrodes or magnetic fields, tFUS can potentially achieve millimetre-scale spatial targeting while delivering energy through the intact skull. The development of a practical tFUS system, however, is not simply a matter of selecting an ultrasound transducer and applying an electrical signal. The acoustic beam must be designed, simulated, experimentally characterized, and eventually optimized for propagation through the skull. My work on the development of a tFUS device therefore follows a complete engineering pipeline—from transducer design and acoustic modelling to experimental validation and automated beam characterization.

The starting point of the system is a focused piezoelectric transducer. A spherical-cap geometry is particularly attractive for tFUS because the curved aperture naturally converges acoustic waves toward a focal region. The geometry of the transducer strongly influences its acoustic behaviour. Parameters such as aperture diameter, radius of curvature, focal length, operating frequency, and piezoelectric material affect the focal position, focal dimensions, acoustic pressure, beam divergence, and sidelobe structure. For the experimental system, a focused PZT transducer is used to generate the acoustic field. The transducer is driven by an electrical excitation generated using a waveform generator and amplified before being applied to the piezoelectric element. Pulsed excitation is particularly important for tFUS because it provides control over pulse duration, pulse repetition frequency, duty cycle, and acoustic exposure. The electrical-to-acoustic conversion process is therefore an important part of the overall device architecture: waveform generation → power amplification → piezoelectric excitation → acoustic propagation → focused pressure field.

Focused Ultrasound Acoustic Field Scan

Before experimental testing, the acoustic field can be investigated numerically using finite-element modelling. A major part of the development process involves building an axisymmetric model of the spherical-cap transducer in COMSOL Multiphysics. The model allows the relationship between transducer geometry and the resulting acoustic field to be studied without having to manufacture and test every possible configuration. The simulated field provides important quantities such as acoustic pressure distribution, focal position, focal width, focal length, and beam profile. This modelling stage is particularly useful for understanding how changes in parameters such as aperture size, focal length, excitation frequency, and acoustic boundary conditions influence the focus. The eventual objective is not simply to obtain a visually appealing simulated focus. The simulation must provide quantitative predictions that can be compared against experimental measurements.

Experimental Characterization Using a Hydrophone

The next stage is experimental characterization. The acoustic field is measured inside a water tank using a hydrophone. Instead of assuming that the transducer produces the beam predicted by simulation, the hydrophone provides a direct measurement of the acoustic pressure generated by the physical device. A motorized three-axis scanning system is used to move the hydrophone through the acoustic field. By recording the hydrophone signal at different spatial positions, the pressure distribution can be reconstructed. This transforms the measurement process from a single-point measurement into a three-dimensional beam-characterization system. The measured voltage from the hydrophone can be converted into acoustic pressure using the calibrated hydrophone sensitivity. From the pressure waveform, quantities such as peak pressure and acoustic intensity can subsequently be calculated.

Automated Three-Axis Beam Scanning

One of the key engineering components of the project is the development of an automated XYZ scanning system. The scanning mechanism uses stepper motors and motor drivers controlled through an embedded controller. The hydrophone can therefore be moved systematically through the acoustic field according to a predefined scanning pattern. Raster and serpentine scanning strategies can be used to acquire two-dimensional beam maps efficiently. By combining motion data with synchronized hydrophone measurements, the system can reconstruct axial and transverse beam profiles. The resulting data can then be visualized as 2D and 3D acoustic maps. An important aspect of this development is automation. Rather than manually positioning the hydrophone and recording individual measurements, the scanning platform provides a repeatable method for characterizing the transducer.

Simulation–Experiment Correlation

A major objective of the device-development process is to establish agreement between numerical simulations and experimental measurements. The COMSOL model predicts the acoustic field, while the hydrophone-based scanning system measures the physical field. Comparing the two allows discrepancies to be identified. Differences can arise from several sources, including transducer manufacturing tolerances, material properties, electrical impedance, mounting conditions, hydrophone response, reflections, alignment errors, and simplifications in the numerical model. Instead of treating these differences simply as errors, they provide information that can be used to improve the model and refine the experimental setup. This creates an iterative engineering loop: Design → Simulation → Fabrication → Measurement → Validation → Optimization Such a loop is essential for developing a reliable ultrasound system.
