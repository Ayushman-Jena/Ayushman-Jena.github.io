Standard Flight Trajectory Simulation of Sounding Rockets

Ayushman Jena September 2026 12 min read Rocketry Flight Dynamics Simulation Control

A sounding rocket trajectory is fundamentally an initial-value problem in flight dynamics. Given the rocket's initial state, mass properties, propulsion characteristics, aerodynamic coefficients, atmospheric conditions, and launch geometry, the objective is to determine the position and velocity of the vehicle throughout its flight.

For a small sounding rocket intended to reach approximately \(1-1.5~\mathrm{km}\), a three-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) point-mass model provides a useful first-order representation. The rocket is represented by its translational position and velocity while its attitude is represented through the flight-path direction.

The four principal external effects are thrust, aerodynamic drag, gravity, and—in a more complete model—aerodynamic lift. For a slender sounding rocket flying close to its nominal launch axis, lift can initially be neglected and the trajectory can be described primarily through thrust, drag, and gravity.

Modeling assumption: The K-class solid motor is represented by its experimentally obtained or manufacturer-supplied thrust-time curve. The trajectory model does not attempt to derive the internal grain geometry, chamber design, or nozzle dimensions.

1. Coordinate System

Consider a vertical launch coordinate system with \(x\) representing downrange displacement and \(z\) representing altitude. The rocket position is

\[ \mathbf r = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ z \end{bmatrix} \]

and the velocity vector is

\[ \mathbf v = \begin{bmatrix} v_x\\ v_z \end{bmatrix}. \]

For a launch angle \(\theta\) measured from the local horizontal,

\[ \mathbf v = V \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ v_x = V\cos\theta, \qquad v_z = V\sin\theta. \]

2. Equations of Motion

The translational dynamics follow Newton's second law:

\[ m(t)\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = \mathbf F_T+ \mathbf F_D+ \mathbf F_g. \]

Hence,

\[ \frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = \frac{1}{m(t)} \left( \mathbf F_T+ \mathbf F_D \right) + \mathbf g. \]

This is the basic form used in a 3-DOF rocket trajectory model. NASA's 3-DOF rocket dynamics formulation similarly represents acceleration using thrust, aerodynamic drag, atmospheric density, vehicle mass, and gravity.

The position dynamics are

\[ \frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}=\mathbf v. \]

Therefore, the complete state vector can be written as

\[ \mathbf X = \begin{bmatrix} x & z & v_x & v_z \end{bmatrix}^{T}. \]

The trajectory simulation is consequently an initial-value problem:

\[ \dot{\mathbf X} = f(t,\mathbf X). \]

3. Propulsion Model

For a solid-propellant sounding rocket, the most practical trajectory simulation approach is to use a thrust-time profile \(T(t)\). The thrust is zero before ignition and after motor burnout:

\[ T(t)= \begin{cases} 0, & tt_b \end{cases} \]

where \(t_i\) is ignition time and \(t_b\) is burnout time.

The total impulse of the motor is

\[ I_t = \int_{t_i}^{t_b}T(t)\,dt. \]

The average thrust can therefore be estimated as

\[ \bar T = \frac{I_t}{t_b-t_i}. \]

For trajectory simulation, however, the instantaneous value \(T(t)\) should be retained whenever a measured thrust curve is available. This is particularly important because a solid motor does not generally produce constant thrust.

4. Time-Varying Rocket Mass

During powered flight the rocket mass decreases as propellant is consumed. The instantaneous mass is

\[ m(t)=m_0-m_p(t), \]

where \(m_0\) is the launch mass and \(m_p(t)\) is the mass of propellant consumed by time \(t\).

The propellant mass flow rate is related to thrust through the effective exhaust velocity:

\[ T(t)\approx \dot m_p(t)V_{\mathrm{eq}}(t). \]

Thus, when an appropriate effective exhaust velocity is available,

\[ \dot m_p(t) = \frac{T(t)}{V_{\mathrm{eq}}}. \]

The ideal rocket equation provides the corresponding relationship between mass ratio and ideal velocity increment:

\[ \Delta V_{\mathrm{ideal}} = V_{\mathrm{eq}} \ln \left( \frac{m_0}{m_f} \right). \] \]

This is an idealized propulsion relation; the actual trajectory velocity is lower because gravitational and aerodynamic losses consume part of the available velocity increment.

5. Thrust Vector

Assuming that the thrust vector is aligned with the rocket longitudinal axis, the thrust components are

\[ T_x=T\cos\theta, \qquad T_z=T\sin\theta. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf F_T = T \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

During the initial rail-constrained portion of the flight, \(\theta\) can be treated as the launch-rail angle. After rail exit, the flight-path angle evolves according to the translational dynamics.

6. Aerodynamic Drag

Aerodynamic drag is one of the dominant non-gravitational forces during the low-altitude flight of a sounding rocket. NASA expresses aerodynamic drag in the standard form

\[ D= \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 C_D A, \]

where

  • \(\rho\) is atmospheric density,
  • \(V\) is rocket airspeed,
  • \(C_D\) is the drag coefficient, and
  • \(A\) is the reference cross-sectional area.

The drag vector acts opposite to the velocity vector:

\[ \mathbf F_D = -\frac{1}{2} \rho C_D A V\mathbf v. \]

This vector form is particularly convenient for numerical integration because it automatically applies drag opposite to the instantaneous direction of motion.

7. Mach-Dependent Drag Coefficient

For a realistic sounding-rocket simulation, \(C_D\) should not necessarily be treated as constant.

The Mach number is

\[ M= \frac{V}{a}, \]

where \(a\) is the local speed of sound:

\[ a= \sqrt{\gamma R T}. \]

A practical aerodynamic model therefore uses a lookup table

\[ C_D=C_D(M). \]

The drag coefficient can be obtained from wind-tunnel measurements, CFD, or a validated aerodynamic model. For a first simulation, a representative constant \(C_D\) can be used, followed by a Mach-dependent model for higher-fidelity analysis.

8. Atmospheric Model

Since the intended trajectory remains within the lower atmosphere, atmospheric density changes significantly enough that assuming constant density introduces a measurable error.

A standard atmospheric model can be used to obtain \(T(h)\), \(p(h)\), and \(\rho(h)\). The U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 provides atmospheric properties over a wide altitude range and is commonly used for trajectory and flight-dynamics simulations.

For the lower atmosphere, the temperature can be approximated by

\[ T(h)=T_0-Lh, \]

where \(L\) is the temperature lapse rate.

The pressure is obtained from

\[ p(h)= p_0 \left( \frac{T(h)}{T_0} \right)^{\frac{g_0M_a}{RL}}, \]

and density follows from the ideal-gas equation:

\[ \rho(h)= \frac{p(h)} {R_{\mathrm{air}}T(h)}. \]

NASA also provides equivalent metric atmospheric curve fits in which pressure and temperature are functions of altitude and density is obtained from the equation of state.

9. Gravity Variation

For a trajectory of only \(1-1.5~\mathrm{km}\), using \(g=9.80665~\mathrm{m/s^2}\) is normally an excellent first approximation.

For a more complete trajectory model, gravitational acceleration can be expressed as

\[ g(h)= g_0 \left( \frac{R_E}{R_E+h} \right)^2, \]

where \(R_E\) is the mean Earth radius.

At a few kilometres of altitude, the variation is small, but using \(g(h)\) makes the model physically consistent and becomes useful when the same simulation framework is later extended to higher altitude vehicles.

10. Complete 2D Equations of Motion

Combining thrust, drag and gravity gives the translational equations

\[ m\frac{dv_x}{dt} = T\cos\theta - \frac{1}{2} \rho C_D A Vv_x, \]
\[ m\frac{dv_z}{dt} = T\sin\theta - \frac{1}{2} \rho C_D A Vv_z - mg. \]

The position equations are

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=v_x \]
\[ \frac{dz}{dt}=v_z. \]

with

\[ V= \sqrt{v_x^2+v_z^2}. \]

After burnout, \(T=0\), and the equations automatically reduce to the ballistic-plus-drag equations.

11. Flight-Path Angle

After the rocket leaves the launch rail, the flight-path angle can be obtained directly from the velocity components:

\[ \gamma= \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{v_z}{v_x} \right). \]

The trajectory therefore becomes

\[ \dot{x}=V\cos\gamma, \qquad \dot{z}=V\sin\gamma. \]

This representation is particularly useful for plotting the trajectory in the familiar altitude-versus-downrange coordinate system.

12. Launch-Rail Dynamics

The rocket is constrained by the launch rail until rail exit. During this interval, the rocket velocity is approximately aligned with the rail.

For a rail angle \(\theta_0\), the simplified one-dimensional equation along the rail is

\[ m\frac{dV}{dt} = T(t) - D - mg\sin\theta_0. \]

The corresponding rail position is

\[ \frac{ds}{dt}=V. \]

The simulation switches to free-flight dynamics once \[ s\geq L_{\mathrm{rail}}. \]

13. Numerical Integration

The equations do not generally possess a convenient closed-form solution because thrust, mass, atmospheric density, drag coefficient, and velocity all vary with time.

Consequently, numerical integration is used. A fourth-order Runge-Kutta method can be written as

\[ \mathbf X_{k+1} = \mathbf X_k+ \frac{\Delta t}{6} \left( \mathbf k_1+ 2\mathbf k_2+ 2\mathbf k_3+ \mathbf k_4 \right), \]

where

\[ \mathbf k_1=f(t_k,\mathbf X_k), \] \[ \mathbf k_2= f\left( t_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2}, \mathbf X_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf k_1 \right), \] \[ \mathbf k_3= f\left( t_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2}, \mathbf X_k+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf k_2 \right), \] \[ \mathbf k_4= f(t_k+\Delta t,\mathbf X_k+\Delta t\mathbf k_3). \]

For a short-duration sounding-rocket flight, a sufficiently small integration step is important during motor ignition, rapid acceleration and burnout.

14. Detecting Apogee

Apogee occurs when the vertical component of velocity changes sign. Therefore, the apogee condition is

\[ v_z(t_a)=0. \]

Numerically, if

\[ v_z(t_k)>0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad v_z(t_{k+1})\leq0, \]

then the simulation has crossed apogee.

A simple interpolation gives an estimate of the exact apogee time:

\[ t_a \approx t_k+ \frac{v_{z,k}} {v_{z,k}-v_{z,k+1}} \Delta t. \]

The corresponding altitude is then obtained by interpolating \(z(t)\).

15. Key Performance Parameters

A standard sounding-rocket trajectory simulation should report at least the following quantities:

Primary outputs: maximum altitude, time to apogee, burnout altitude, burnout velocity, maximum velocity, maximum acceleration, downrange displacement, and total flight time.

The maximum velocity is

\[ V_{\max} = \max_t \sqrt{v_x^2+v_z^2}. \]

The maximum acceleration is

\[ a_{\max} = \max_t \left\| \frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} \right\|. \]

The maximum dynamic pressure is

\[ q= \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2, \qquad q_{\max}=\max_t q. \]

Dynamic pressure is especially useful when evaluating aerodynamic loading on a slender sounding rocket.

16. Representative VSLV-Class Simulation Architecture

For a VSLV-type student sounding-rocket simulation targeting approximately \(1-1.5~\mathrm{km}\), the model can be organized into five coupled subsystems:

  1. Propulsion: \(T(t)\), propellant mass flow and burnout.
  2. Mass Properties: \(m(t)\), center of gravity and inertia.
  3. Aerodynamics: \(C_D(M)\), reference area and drag.
  4. Atmosphere: \(\rho(h)\), \(T(h)\), \(p(h)\) and \(a(h)\).
  5. Dynamics: translational equations and numerical integration.

The computational architecture can therefore be represented as

\[ T(t) \rightarrow \boxed{\text{Propulsion}} \rightarrow m(t) \]
\[ \left[ T(t),m(t),C_D(M),A,\rho(h),g(h) \right] \rightarrow \boxed{\text{6/3-DOF Dynamics}} \rightarrow \left[ x,z,v_x,v_z \right]. \]

The trajectory is then obtained by integrating this state forward in time.

17. Flight Phases

The complete flight can be divided into several physical phases.

17.1 Ignition and Rail Phase

The motor develops thrust while the rocket remains mechanically constrained by the launch rail. The primary objective of this phase is to establish sufficient velocity before rail exit.

17.2 Powered Flight

After rail exit, the rocket accelerates under the combined action of thrust, drag and gravity:

\[ \mathbf a = \frac{\mathbf T+\mathbf D}{m} + \mathbf g. \]

17.3 Burnout

At \(t=t_b\),

\[ T(t_b)=0. \]

The rocket then enters the ballistic coast phase.

17.4 Coast to Apogee

During ascent after burnout, the vertical velocity decreases due primarily to gravity and aerodynamic drag until

\[ v_z=0. \]

This defines apogee.

17.5 Descent

After apogee, \(v_z<0\). If the rocket deploys a recovery system, the aerodynamic model must be changed to represent the parachute configuration.

18. Simplified Analytical Check

Before trusting a numerical simulation, it is useful to compare it with an idealized ballistic model.

If the rocket leaves the powered phase with purely vertical velocity \(V_b\), and drag is neglected, the additional altitude above burnout is approximately

\[ \Delta h_{\mathrm{coast}} = \frac{V_b^2}{2g}. \]

The ideal time from burnout to apogee is

\[ t_{\mathrm{coast}} = \frac{V_b}{g}. \]

Thus the approximate apogee becomes

\[ h_{\max} \approx h_b+ \frac{V_b^2}{2g}. \]

These expressions are not expected to reproduce the real trajectory exactly because aerodynamic drag has been neglected, but they provide an important sanity check. NASA gives the same fundamental ballistic relationship for vertical motion.

19. From 3-DOF to 6-DOF

A 3-DOF model treats the rocket primarily as a point mass. A higher-fidelity VSLV simulation can instead use a full six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body model.

The translational state becomes

\[ \mathbf X_T= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf r\\ \mathbf v \end{bmatrix}, \]

while rotational motion is described using angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and an attitude representation such as quaternions:

\[ \mathbf q= \begin{bmatrix} q_0&q_1&q_2&q_3 \end{bmatrix}^{T}. \]

The rotational dynamics are governed by Euler's rigid-body equation:

\[ \mathbf I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} + \boldsymbol\omega \times \left( \mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega \right) = \mathbf M. \]

This introduces aerodynamic moments, thrust misalignment, center-of-gravity motion, inertia variation and control-system dynamics.

NASA's POST and related flight-dynamics tools support both 3-DOF and 6-DOF trajectory formulations, illustrating the natural progression from point-mass trajectory analysis to full vehicle dynamics.

20. Simulation Workflow

A practical computational workflow is therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Rocket Parameters} } \rightarrow \boxed{ T(t),m(t),C_D(M) } \rightarrow \boxed{ \rho(h),T_{\mathrm{atm}}(h),g(h) } \rightarrow \boxed{ \text{Equations of Motion} } \rightarrow \boxed{ \text{Numerical Integration} } \rightarrow \boxed{ \text{Trajectory} } } \]

The resulting state history can then be used to generate:

  • Altitude vs. time
  • Downrange distance vs. time
  • Altitude vs. downrange trajectory
  • Velocity vs. time
  • Acceleration vs. time
  • Dynamic pressure vs. time
  • Mach number vs. time
  • Apogee and burnout markers

21. Validation Against Flight-Computer Data

A particularly useful extension for a student sounding rocket is comparing the simulated trajectory against the onboard flight computer.

If the flight computer contains an IMU and barometric pressure sensor, the measured state can be compared with the simulated trajectory:

\[ \mathbf X_{\mathrm{sim}}(t) \quad\text{vs.}\quad \mathbf X_{\mathrm{meas}}(t). \]

The altitude error can be defined as

\[ e_h(t) = h_{\mathrm{sim}}(t) - h_{\mathrm{meas}}(t). \]

Similarly, the root-mean-square altitude error is

\[ e_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( h_{\mathrm{sim},k} - h_{\mathrm{meas},k} \right)^2 }. \]

This converts the trajectory simulator from a purely predictive model into a system-identification and flight-validation tool.

22. Conclusion

A standard sounding-rocket trajectory simulation can be built from a relatively compact mathematical model. The essential dynamics are governed by time-varying mass, thrust, aerodynamic drag, atmospheric density and gravity.

For a VSLV-class student sounding rocket targeting approximately \(1-1.5~\mathrm{km}\), the recommended progression is to first establish a validated 3-DOF model using the actual motor thrust-time data, measured vehicle mass and experimentally or computationally determined aerodynamic coefficients.

Once this model reproduces flight data satisfactorily, the same framework can be extended to a 6-DOF rigid-body simulation with attitude dynamics, aerodynamic moments, wind, sensor models and flight-computer estimation.

Key idea: The trajectory is not determined by thrust alone. A realistic sounding-rocket trajectory emerges from the coupled interaction of propulsion, changing mass, gravity, atmospheric density, aerodynamics and the rocket's initial conditions.
